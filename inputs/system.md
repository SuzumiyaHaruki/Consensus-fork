This document defines the system deliverables and guides implementation and validation of `protocol.md`. The protocol and required service behavior constrain the result; the design questions below guide your reasoning without prescribing a runtime architecture or data structure.

Separate required behavior, external assumptions, ideal-model results and implementation choices in the design. Choose mechanisms left open by the materials and continue without waiting for a researcher to select an answer. Explain consequential choices, their operating conditions and observable costs, and check that they preserve required behavior. Multiple viable mechanisms do not constitute a specification gap. Report a gap when missing or contradictory required behavior prevents judging compliance; uncertainty about your implementation alone calls for investigation. Do not silently narrow a requirement or strengthen an external assumption to make the implementation appear correct.

## Service scope

Deliver a Go module containing a reusable consensus node and a replicated key/value service. Nodes must run as separate processes with configurable identity, fixed membership, addresses, and Delta. Provide a real network transport, client CLI tooling supporting multiple independent concurrent client instances, and a local launch/cleanup script or equivalent documented commands. Client tooling is a deliverable, not a limit of one client, connection or outstanding request. Use loopback by default. A production deployment platform and a general storage engine are unnecessary.

Implement deterministic Put, Get, and Delete operations on individual keys. Document conflict classification and the service consistency guarantee justified by the protocol and integration. Follow the execution-before-response rule in protocol section 12 and the client/result requirements below. A complete client-history linearizability guarantee has not been established in these materials; returning only after execution and eventual replica agreement do not by themselves establish it. Identify the exact gap if a claimed service guarantee cannot be justified. Read operations must follow a documented consistency path, not silently read arbitrary local state.

## Runtime and network integration

The node owns background progress after startup. Applications must not manually tick time or pump each protocol transition in normal operation. Safely support concurrent submissions, incoming messages, deadlines and application callbacks. Choose clock, scheduling and synchronization mechanisms that preserve the protocol's elapsed-time and progress requirements. Explain blocking/reentry restrictions, ownership of input bytes, overload behavior, and shutdown of pending operations and background resources.

Relate the protocol's abstract sending and reception events to actual transport and Node operations. Explain what the chosen boundaries establish, how partial broadcasts and failed attempts affect pending work, and why completed sends satisfy their delivery obligation even if the sender subsequently crashes. Check compatible meanings across components and progress when a target has already crashed. Choose the mechanism yourself; additional exchanges must preserve message meanings, decision conditions and the failure model. Renaming an event does not remove intervening processing costs or excuse discarding a timely valid reply.

For each protocol wait, identify its actual starting event, expiry condition and the work needed for a reply or state update to affect that check. Distinguish prescribed waits, ideal-model aggregate results and actual elapsed time as in protocol section 13. Account for sending, processing and scheduling in your implementation's timing explanation; compare it with observed events. Extra elapsed time alone does not violate an ideal aggregate bound, but still investigate whether it causes false death facts or prevents progress. Dependency waiting and client completion remain separate. Measurements support the tested conditions, not a universal real-time bound; uncertainty about a derived bound does not by itself require a researcher to choose a mechanism.

Investigate how reception and deadline handling interact when their work overlaps, and how protocol and application work continue as new requests arrive. Select event orders that could challenge your design's account of accurate failure detection, atomic transitions or progress, then exercise the relevant handlers. Explain what the observations establish and what remains uncertain. No specific reception or scheduling mechanism is prescribed; assess your chosen mechanism against the protocol and service behavior.

Preserve message identity and payload exactly across the wire. Where the materials leave representation or configuration ranges open, choose and validate supported values; accepted values must work consistently across components. This does not authorize restricting an explicitly required domain or changing a limit after admission to excuse a failure. Document codec behavior and sender binding for the trusted CFT deployment. Bind a connection/message source to its configured member identity; an unverified payload field is not authentication. A local trusted-cluster mode is sufficient when its trust boundary is explicit; this task does not demand Byzantine tolerance or a production PKI.

Execute dependency-ready commands while requests continue. Preserve dependency precedence, deterministic order inside SCCs, and execution deduplication. Ready independent work must not be indefinitely displaced by newly ready work. ## Client submissions and results

Provide a reusable client interface and CLI following the conforming client model in protocol section 14. Multiple clients may issue concurrent operations; each logical operation is submitted at most once to one selected replica. Do not retry, fail over to another replica to resubmit, or turn an uncertain operation into a new command. Successful service operation responses require actual execution and retention of the application result as specified in protocol section 12; receipt, admission, or metadata decision alone is insufficient.

Implement the command-number allocation in `protocol.md` section 14. One allocator owns a ClientID for its lifetime and assigns a different RequestSeq to each new operation. Independent allocators need different ClientIDs; concurrent callers sharing an allocator must allocate sequence numbers safely. Preserve `(ClientID, RequestSeq)` exactly through string or numeric conversions and use the specified comparison inside SCCs. Multiple clients may use the same sequence number under different ClientIDs. Support out-of-order requests and sequence gaps without requiring serial client operation or waiting for absent lower numbers.

Construct the complete request and select its original coordinator before submission. A fresh allocation session suffices for new operations after a client restart; do not restore or resubmit old pending operations. Client request journals, saved-request loading, cross-replica result queries, and client restart recovery are not required. Explicitly assigned distinct ClientIDs are sufficient for controlled experiments. The client remains a usable concurrent workload entry point; real networking and autonomous node operation remain required.

Reject malformed input before admitting it. If a known command ID arrives with different operation contents or a different original coordinator, reject that input while preserving the existing command, its retained result, and other legal commands' progress. A rejected input must not become a dependency. If different replicas have already accepted different contents for the same ID, follow section 14's model limits: report the violation and its service impact; local rejection does not choose a global winner or restore service. Keep local acceptance, metadata decision, dependency readiness, application result, and successful client response distinct.

Distinguish rejection before admission, a retained execution outcome (success or application error), and an unknown outcome in the client API and CLI. If temporary rejection such as overload is exposed, state whether it guarantees the command was not admitted. An application error is an execution result, not permission to execute the command again. A new Get of the current value is a new logical operation with a new identity. Transport retransmission and replica messages may repeat knowledge of a command even without client retries: preserve atomic first observation, existing command flows, watchdog deadlines, and per-replica execution deduplication. Do not claim exactly-once external side effects or durable results after a replica restart.

A timeout, disconnect, or cancelled client wait without a definite outcome means that the result is unknown to that caller; it does not imply the operation never committed or executed, and it does not cancel an admitted command's protocol or execution obligations. End the wait without resubmitting. Choose and document a client waiting policy separately from protocol failure detection: a client timeout neither confirms a replica dead nor authorizes a new command identity or coordinator. No universal client completion deadline is specified. The metadata decision bounds exclude dependency recovery and readiness, execution, and client communication, so they cannot directly serve as an end-to-end completion guarantee.

Do not require another client submission to activate replica discovery or takeover. Under the protocol's assumptions, a complete original that reaches a permanently correct replica remains subject to the metadata progress requirements; execution also needs the stated dependency readiness. A request that never reaches a permanently correct replica has no eventual decision guarantee. If the contacted replica crashes before replying, loss of the client response is permitted even if the command executes elsewhere; returning that result through another replica is outside this scope. Normal fault-free operation must still return execution outcomes to connected waiting clients. Observe surviving replicas' decisions and executions separately from client responses when investigating progress.

## Fault model and observability

Support fixed membership and at most f cumulative crash-stop failures as specified. A crashed replica never resumes or rejoins this configuration; preserving its memory for restart, restart recovery and catch-up are outside the task, so crash-stop does not require a WAL. Fault injection must stop that replica's future protocol actions permanently while preserving the delivery obligations of already completed sends. Holding and releasing a test event within the timing assumptions is scheduling control, not a crash followed by recovery. Do not claim arbitrary partition tolerance, safe deletion of retained history or snapshot installation. Retaining history in memory for the lifetime of a surviving process is acceptable; document its growth and resulting service limit.

Provide documented control and observation points for investigating actual Node behavior. Choose replaceable dependencies or narrow hooks that let a test hold or release relevant external events while exercising the Node's own queue handling, timer dispatch, send-completion handling and application integration. The test should enter through the supported Node/service interfaces and let the affected implementation handlers run; it must not supply their state transitions. Include a short runnable example, which may be an existing test with its invocation, chosen from interactions between mechanisms the implementation actually uses. Explain what each mechanism assumes about the others and exercise their relevant interaction; isolated component checks may miss incompatible completion, timing or blocking behavior. Identify the event held and released, how its occurrence is observed, which runtime components run unchanged, which are substituted or bypassed, and any effect instrumentation has on ordering or blocking. If the relevant transport or runtime is bypassed, state what remains to be checked on that path. A future reviewer should be able to adapt this example without inventing a second runtime. Core-level tests remain useful for protocol transitions, but do not establish runtime behavior. Choose the interfaces and scenarios yourself; no fixed Node architecture, test inventory, or control of every goroutine is required.

Expose observations sufficient to relate command identity, sender/recipient, message kind, completion, decisions, execution, local retirement, and stop events. Retirement must be attributable to actual local execution completion. Distinguish requested test actions from events actually reached. Observation hooks must report what the implementation did, rather than return the test driver's expected outcome. Do not require a specific event schema or build an alternative protocol implementation for testing.

Use the same declared send, reception and deadline meanings in the implementation, design and runtime tests. A Core-only model may simplify these events, but must identify the abstraction rather than claim it tested the real Node boundary. Implementation choices may change during repair if their explanations and tests are updated and supplied requirements remain satisfied. Real-time experiments must state their timing assumptions. Delay beyond Delta, overload beyond a stated processing bound, or unsupported restarts cannot silently become counterexamples to a narrower guarantee. Conversely, an implementation choice or declared limit cannot excuse a failure to meet the supplied assumptions or behavior; identify that gap instead of hiding it as a generic network failure.
